# Reward

This file is why a person posts again, stays, or leaves. Ranking lives in `ranking.md`.

## Likes move the next post

Lindström and colleagues, Nature Communications, 2021. File: `research/papers/08-lindstrom-2021-reward-learning.pdf`.

They modelled posting as a free-operant response followed by likes. Across four platforms, 1,046,857 posts from 4,168 people, likes Granger-caused the time until the next post. People posted sooner when the recent rate of likes was higher, and waited longer when it was lower. The fit is the quantitative law of effect: response rate rises with reward rate and then flattens (Instagram mean R² 0.43; the three forums 0.37). The model balances two costs. Posting has an effort cost, so bursting is punished. Not posting has an opportunity cost that rises with the reward rate, so a hot streak pulls the next post forward. An online experiment (n = 176) changed the reward rate and the latencies moved with it.

Use this as the mechanism. A like, a reply, or a save is a social reward. It changes when the person comes back. It does not make the content true, and it does not make the next view predictable.

## Six features that prolong use

Montag, Lachmann, Herrlich, and Zweig, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 2019. CC BY. File: `research/papers/01-montag-2019-addictive-features.pdf`.

A review of design in social apps and freemium games, not a trial. The business model they describe is attention and data in place of a fee. Six features:

- Endless scroll and autoplay. The next item starts before a stopping point. Intermittent rewards, as in a slot machine, keep the scroll going.
- Endowment and mere exposure. Time sunk in a profile, a farm, or a streak makes leaving feel like a loss. Repeated exposure itself increases liking.
- Social pressure. WhatsApp's double tick tells both sides a message was read. The default is on. The paper links this to pressure to answer fast, and to fear of missing out.
- A feed that shows people what they already hover on, including mood inferred from text.
- Social comparison and the like. Instagram photos shown with many likes drove stronger ventral-striatum response than the same photos with few likes. Leaderboards do the same job in games.
- The Zeigarnik effect. Interrupted tasks are remembered and resumed. Candy Crush spends lives on a "super hard" level and sells the next life.

The authors say a single feature is hard to isolate without platform data, and they argue for regulating these elements rather than copying them. Do not ship them as a growth plan. A marketer may name one if it is already in the product, and say what it costs the person.

## Leaving the app feels better than people predict

Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer, and Gentzkow, NBER Working Paper 25514, revised November 2019. File: `research/papers/12-allcott-2019-welfare-facebook.pdf`. This copy is local only. Do not commit it.

They recruited 2,743 US Facebook users before the 2018 midterms, measured what they would take to deactivate, and paid a random subset of those with a price under $102 to stay off for four weeks. Deactivation reduced other online activity and increased offline time, including TV alone and time with family and friends. It reduced factual news knowledge and political polarization. Subjective well-being went up. Use stayed lower after the four weeks, and the price people put on Facebook fell, which the authors read as evidence that use inflates how much the product seems worth.

Use this when a brief asks for "hooks that make them unable to leave." The experiment is the evidence that time-on-site is not the same as welfare. Write the hook that earns a real next step. Do not write a plan whose success metric is only minutes captured.
